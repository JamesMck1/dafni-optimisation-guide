Section 1 · Introduction

What is the hydrodynamic optimisation tool?

The hydrodynamic optimisation tool is a decision-support workflow for identifying effective combinations of blue-green infrastructure to reduce urban flood risk. It combines the CityCAT hydrodynamic model with a bespoke epsilon-dominance multi-objective evolutionary algorithm (ε-MOEA).

Rather than testing one intervention plan at a time, the tool repeatedly generates candidate strategies, simulates their flood response and learns which options offer the strongest trade-offs. The result is a set of Pareto-optimal solutions: strategies for which one objective cannot be improved without compromising another.

The workflow has been containerised and deployed on the Data & Analytics Facility for National Infrastructure (DAFNI), providing a reproducible environment for computationally intensive studies.

Audience

Who is this guide for?

This guide is for people who want to explore where flood-management interventions could be placed and how competing objectives might be balanced. It supports both technical users and decision-makers who will interpret the results.

01

Flood-risk authorities

Lead Local Flood Authorities and Environment Agency teams assessing strategic options.

02

Water-sector practitioners

Water companies and consultants developing urban drainage and resilience strategies.

03

Planners and decision-makers

People comparing investment priorities and communicating cost-risk trade-offs.

04

Researchers and students

Users investigating hydrodynamic optimisation or blue-green infrastructure planning.

Purpose

What decisions can the tool support?

The tool supports early-stage optioneering. It helps users investigate which intervention types and locations could reduce flood risk, how cost and flood performance trade off, and whether several distinct strategies perform similarly.

  • Compare candidate intervention portfolios across a study area.
  • Explore trade-offs between estimated intervention cost and flood response.
  • Identify spatial patterns shared by high-performing solutions.
  • Provide evidence for stakeholder discussion and detailed appraisal.

Design space

What interventions can be considered?

A study can define candidate locations for several types of blue-green infrastructure and flood-management feature. The optimisation algorithm uses fixed Gray binary coding to represent whether each candidate intervention is selected.

Permeable paving

Surfacing designed to promote infiltration and reduce surface runoff.

Rain gardens

Vegetated features that collect, store and manage runoff.

Green-area interventions

Defined green infrastructure options represented within the study design space.

Detention features

Flexible basins, fixed flood-management features and offline storage connected through inlets.

Study-specific candidates

The options are determined by the intervention datasets supplied for the study. The optimiser selects among these candidates; it does not independently identify buildable sites.

Optimisation objectives

Which objectives are available?

Available

Outlet hydrograph

Evaluates simulated flow at a defined outlet using a hydrograph-based performance measure.

Available

Property flood risk

Evaluates flooding at defined property locations to compare property-level exposure.

Not available

Economic damages

This objective remains under development because building types cannot yet be assigned automatically and reliably.

Choose only supported objectives

Do not configure a study around the damages objective at this stage. The guide will be updated when the required building-classification workflow is available.

Prerequisites

What do I need before starting?

Users need access to DAFNI and a CityCAT-ready study. At minimum, the hydrodynamic model requires rainfall, a digital elevation model (DEM) and topography data.

  • DAFNI access and permission to use the workflow and required compute resources.
  • Rainfall data representing the event or scenario to be modelled.
  • A digital elevation model covering the study domain.
  • Topography data describing the urban environment for CityCAT.
  • Candidate intervention data defining the measures and locations the optimiser may select.
  • A clear objective and an agreed basis for interpreting cost and flood performance.
Mandatory model inputs

Rainfall, DEM and topography are required. Later sections will explain the expected files, coordinate system and folder structure; spatial inputs should use British National Grid (EPSG:27700).

Results

What will the tool produce?

Each run records the optimisation and the strategies it discovered. The principal result is the Pareto front, showing non-dominated trade-offs between objectives.

Pareto-front plots

Visual summaries of the best trade-offs found during the optimisation.

Solution records

A Solutions.txt file containing encoded strategies and their objective values.

Spatial solution views

Maps or plots showing the interventions selected in individual solutions.

Run provenance

Parameters, unique simulation count, execution log, and archived inputs and setup.

Planned capability: restart and recovery

Recovery modules are largely prepared to continue optimisation from solutions generated by an earlier run. Full integration is on hold, so restart and recovery are shown as a placeholder rather than an available workflow.

Use with care

Important limitations

  • Optioneering, not detailed design. Results identify promising strategies for further investigation, not construction-ready designs.
  • Results reflect the model. Conclusions depend on inputs, model setup, calibration and rainfall scenario.
  • Cost assumptions matter. Estimates should be reviewed for the study context and interpreted alongside uncertainty.
  • The search is finite. Outcomes depend on the simulation budget and do not prove that every strategy has been explored.
  • Practical constraints require review. Ownership, utilities, maintenance, ecology, accessibility and constructability are not assessed automatically.

Runtime

Computational requirements

Hydrodynamic optimisation is demanding because every candidate strategy requires a CityCAT simulation. Use a small test run to confirm the setup before committing resources to a production study. Depending on the domain, model resolution, rainfall duration and search budget, a production run may take several days or longer.

DAFNI supplies the managed computational environment, while users remain responsible for selecting an appropriate run size and checking study results.

How to use the guide

About this guide

The guide follows a practical study sequence: understand the method, prepare the CityCAT model and intervention data, configure the DAFNI workflow, run a small validation study, launch the optimisation, and interpret and export the results.

Unavailable or incomplete features are labelled clearly. Instructions describe the deployed tool as it currently works rather than relying on the older repository README.

Section 2 · How the tool works

From intervention strategy to Pareto front

This section explains the optimisation process conceptually. Users do not need to understand the underlying code or evolutionary-computing theory to operate the tool, but understanding the workflow will make its settings and results easier to interpret.

The core process

2.1 The optimisation loop

The tool searches for effective blue-green infrastructure strategies by repeatedly linking the evolutionary algorithm with CityCAT.

  1. Generate a strategy. The evolutionary algorithm creates an encoded combination of interventions.
  2. Decode the chromosome. Gray-coded values are converted into intervention states and characteristics.
  3. Construct the model. The corresponding CityCAT input files are generated or modified.
  4. Run CityCAT. The selected rainfall event is simulated for the candidate strategy.
  5. Analyse flooding. The required hydrograph or property-exposure result is extracted.
  6. Calculate cost. The estimated whole-life cost of the selected interventions is calculated.
  7. Update the archive. The strategy is compared with the high-performing solutions found so far.
  8. Check progress. The tool either generates another set of candidates or returns the final Pareto front.

↻ The cycle repeats until a stopping condition is met.

Why runs are computationally demanding

Every unique candidate requires a hydrodynamic simulation. CityCAT evaluation therefore accounts for most of the computational cost of an optimisation.

Encoding the design space

2.2 How intervention strategies are represented

A complete intervention strategy is represented by a chromosome: a single sequence of binary values assembled from all candidate interventions in the study.

Zonal

Include or exclude

A zonal intervention has two states: 0 excludes it and 1 includes it. This suits predefined permeable-paving, rain-garden and green-area zones.

1 0 1 0

In this example, zones 1 and 3 are included while zones 2 and 4 are excluded.

Local

Configure a feature

Local features can use several groups of bits to encode inclusion, storage depth, area or radius, position within an allowable area, and other feature-specific characteristics.

Keep the design space meaningful

Each zone should represent a distinct physical decision. Overlapping or redundant zones enlarge the search space without creating genuinely different strategies and can make the search less efficient. Encoded dimensions and locations are for optioneering and still require detailed engineering design.

Genetic representation

2.3 Why Gray coding is used

The deployed tool uses Gray binary coding for characteristics with multiple possible values. In standard binary coding, two neighbouring values can have substantially different bit patterns, so several bits may need to change simultaneously. This is known as a Hamming cliff and can hinder evolutionary search.

Gray coding orders the representation so that neighbouring encoded values differ by only one bit. A small genetic change is therefore more likely to produce a small change in the corresponding intervention characteristic.

No user setting is required

Gray coding is fixed within the deployed workflow and does not need to be selected or configured by the user.

Hydrodynamic evaluation

2.4 Evaluating a candidate strategy

Every unique chromosome is converted into a CityCAT model configuration. Depending on the selected interventions, this may change surface permeability and infiltration properties, rain-garden locations, the elevation model used for detention features, fixed flood-management features, or offline storage and inlet arrangements.

CityCAT simulates the supplied rainfall event using this modified configuration. The tool then extracts the information needed by the chosen flood-performance objective.

  • The chromosome is given a unique simulation identifier.
  • Intervention-specific model inputs are generated from the decoded values.
  • CityCAT produces the corresponding depth, flow and related model outputs.
  • The objective calculator derives the required flood-performance value.
Avoiding duplicate simulations

If the algorithm encounters an identical chromosome again, its existing simulation results can be reused. The reported unique-simulation count therefore records new hydrodynamic evaluations rather than every encounter with a strategy.

Fitness evaluation

2.5 The two optimisation objectives

The deployed workflow performs a bi-objective optimisation: it simultaneously minimises intervention cost and one supported measure of flood performance.

Objective 1

Whole-life intervention cost

The present value of implementing and maintaining the selected strategy. Depending on the intervention type and configured assumptions, this can include construction, excavation, routine and major maintenance, professional fees, decommissioning, design lifespan and discounting.

Objective 2

Flood performance

Either the peak simulated discharge derived from the defined outlet hydrograph or hydrographs, or the number of buildings classified as being at high flood risk using simulated depths around their footprints.

Lower values are preferred

Both objectives are minimised. Cost values are planning estimates used to compare strategies consistently, not contractor quotations. The economic-damages objective is not currently available because automated building-type assignment has not yet been integrated.

Initial boundaries

2.6 Establishing the objective space

At the beginning of a run, the tool evaluates two boundary strategies. The do-minimum strategy excludes the encoded interventions. The do-maximum strategy represents the maximum encoded intervention state.

These simulations provide initial estimates of the cost and flood-performance ranges. The tool uses these bounds to divide the objective space into an epsilon grid. If later solutions extend or dominate the initial boundaries, the grid is recalculated and the archive is checked again.

Important modelling assumption

Adding appropriate interventions should not increase flood risk, and every intervention should have a positive cost. If the do-maximum strategy performs worse than the do-minimum strategy, the tool reports an error because the assumptions used to establish the search space are no longer valid.

Preserving useful solutions

2.7 Pareto dominance and the epsilon archive

The optimisation does not search for one universally “best” strategy. Cost and flood performance conflict: a strategy that achieves a greater flood reduction will often require greater investment.

A solution dominates another when it is no worse for either objective and strictly better for at least one. A solution is Pareto optimal when no evaluated alternative dominates it. Together, the non-dominated solutions form the Pareto front.

The ε-MOEA divides the objective space into boxes and retains no more than one representative from each relevant box. Where several solutions occupy the same box, the tool first checks strict dominance. If none dominates, it retains the solution closest to the preferred corner of the box. This produces a manageable, diverse approximation of the Pareto front rather than an archive crowded with nearly identical results.

Evolutionary search

2.8 How new strategies are generated

The search begins with a small population of candidates and an archive seeded by the boundary solutions. One parent is selected from the archive and another from the current population, balancing exploitation of strong existing strategies with exploration of new possibilities.

Selection

Favours existing high-performing strategies when choosing parents.

One-point crossover

Combines portions of two parent chromosomes to produce offspring.

Bit-flip mutation

Introduces small random changes that help explore the search space.

Archive injection

Reintroduces selected archive members when the population is adaptively resized.

Adaptive population sizing

If progress is insufficient, the tool can increase the population size. Twenty-five per cent of the resized population is seeded from the archive, while the remainder restores diversity and explores new strategies.

Completing the search

2.9 When does the optimisation stop?

Hard limit

Maximum simulations

The run stops when the configured maximum number of unique CityCAT simulations is reached. This provides a firm computational budget.

Adaptive

Self-termination

The deployed tool monitors improvement in the normalised hypervolume covered by the archive over a configurable lag window. It can stop when improvement falls below the convergence threshold and the minimum simulation requirement has been satisfied.

Convergence is not proof of global optimality

Self-termination indicates that the search has stopped making sufficient progress. It does not prove that the exact global Pareto front has been found.

Division of responsibilities

A larger simulation budget gives the algorithm more opportunities to discover improved strategies, but increases runtime. Similarly, requesting a more detailed approximation of the Pareto front may increase the number of useful solutions while requiring additional evaluations.

Key point

The tool is an iterative learning process. It proposes an intervention strategy, evaluates it using a fully dynamic CityCAT simulation, retains useful results and uses those results to guide subsequent simulations. The final output is a diverse set of cost-risk trade-offs for decision-makers to examine, not a single prescribed design.
